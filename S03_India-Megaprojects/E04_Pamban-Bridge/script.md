# E04 script: The Bridge That Stands Up for Ships (DRAFT, awaiting approval)

Narration only; each `### Pnn` block is one recording passage. Facts and tags: `sources/LEDGER.md`.

### P01

Here is a bridge that stands up to let a ship pass. Off the coast of Tamil Nadu, a railway crosses the sea to the island of Rameswaram. And in the middle, a section of the track rises into the air.

### P02

The new Pamban Bridge was inaugurated in April 2025. The Press Information Bureau describes it as India's first vertical lift railway sea bridge. Its lifting section is seventy-two point five metres long and can rise up to seventeen metres, so larger ships can pass beneath.

The whole bridge is about two kilometres long. So only a small part of it moves.

### P03

There is a reason to care that is not about the lift. The sea slowly eats iron and steel. The Railways say the original Pamban bridge, built in nineteen fourteen, was no longer suitable for modern rail demands. The new bridge stands three metres higher than the old one.

### P04

Idea one: a counterweight. Think of an old sash window. A hidden weight balances the glass, so a small push lifts it.

The lifting span is supported by counterweights in four towers, and an electric drive raises it. Reports say it opens in about five and a half minutes, against about thirty for the old manual bridge. Before, a ship waits. After, the track steps aside.

### P05

Idea two: salt. Sea air and spray eat metal. The Press Information Bureau says the bridge uses stainless steel reinforcement, high-grade protective paint, fully welded joints, and a polysiloxane coating against corrosion. Think of a raincoat sealed at every seam.

### P06

Idea three: wind. A lifting span is a big flat surface in a strong wind. Think of a kite: the surface that is steady in a light breeze fights you in a gale.

Reports say train operations are suspended when the wind passes fifty-eight kilometres an hour. So a rule, as much as the steel, keeps the bridge safe. Before the rule, wind is a surprise. After it, wind is an input.

### P07

Now, what India built, and what is unfinished. The bridge was built by Rail Vikas Nigam Limited, in turbulent water, strong winds and a cyclone zone.

Its foundations are made for two tracks. But the top structure carries one line for now. India has built the lift. It has not yet finished the second track.

### P08

Not every bridge lifts. Some stand high above a gorge. Others run long across a bay. A lifting bridge solves one problem: a railway and a ship need the same stretch of water. These are different problems, not a league table.

### P09

A fictional afternoon: a ship is waiting in the channel, and a train is two minutes away. Someone has to decide who goes first. A lifting bridge is steel, and it is also a timetable.

### P10

One idea about people: status quo bias. We keep what works until it stops working. The old bridge, built in 1914, served for about a hundred and ten years. Replacing something that works is a hard decision. Replacing something that has failed is easy, and expensive.

### P11

Three things to remember. A counterweight lets a small drive lift a large span. The sea attacks metal, so seams, coatings and steel type matter. And a rule is part of the bridge.

India built the lift. Now the question is the line: when will the second track be laid?

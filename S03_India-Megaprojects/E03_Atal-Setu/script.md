# E03 script: The Road Across the Sea (DRAFT, awaiting approval)

Narration only; each `### Pnn` block is one recording passage. Facts and tags: `sources/LEDGER.md`.

### P01

Between Mumbai and Navi Mumbai, there is water. For years, crossing it meant a long way around.

Then engineers asked: what if the road simply went over the sea?

### P02

Atal Setu, also called the Mumbai Trans Harbour Link, opened in January 2024. The Press Information Bureau says it is about twenty-one point eight kilometres long, with six lanes, and about sixteen point five kilometres of it is over the sea. It is described as India's longest sea bridge. It cost more than seventeen thousand eight hundred and forty crore rupees.

### P03

There is a reason to care that is not about length. The bridge gives faster connections to both the Mumbai and Navi Mumbai airports. Its financier quoted about twenty minutes for the crossing. The old time was given as sixty minutes in one statement, and one hundred and twenty in another. A saving depends on where you start counting.

### P04

Idea one: standing on mud. Under the sea, the seabed can be soft. Think of a tent peg in wet sand: pull, and it comes out. So sea bridges commonly stand on piles, driven deep until they grip firm ground.

Before the piles, a pier has nothing to stand on. After them, it stands on ground it cannot see.

### P05

Idea two: a stiff, light floor. The longer spans of this bridge use steel orthotropic decks. Think of a flat sheet of card: it bends. Fold it into corrugated card, and the same material becomes stiff.

Ribs under a steel plate do the same. A stiff, light deck can reach farther between piers, so fewer piers have to stand in the water. And because the decks are built in a factory, less of the work has to happen at sea.

### P06

Idea three: salt. Sea air attacks steel the way it rusts a bicycle left near the beach. The bridge was designed with corrosion protection and durability in mind, in a marine environment, and with special measures to protect flamingo habitat on both shores.

### P07

Now, what India built, and what it relied on. India's Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority carried out the project. Japan's development agency, JICA, lent about thirteen thousand six hundred and fifty-eight crore rupees. A Japanese expert recommended the orthotropic deck, which JICA says was used in India for the first time here. According to MMRDA, steel spans were made in workshops in Japan, South Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan and Myanmar.

India built the bridge, with partners for the deck technology and for part of the steel. Both are true.

### P08

Length is one measure. Sixteen point five kilometres is the sea section. The full twenty-one point eight includes the viaducts on land. Other bridges are higher, or lift, or carry trains. These are different problems, not a league table.

### P09

A fictional evening: a commuter leaves Sewri at six. Without the bridge, the road goes around the bay. With it, the road goes over. The bridge does not make the city smaller. It changes which side of the water a family can live on.

### P10

One idea about people: ask, per what? Seventeen thousand eight hundred and forty crore rupees sounds huge. Divide it by the twenty-one point eight kilometres, and it is about eight hundred and eighteen crore rupees for each kilometre. A big number without a denominator is only a feeling.

### P11

Three things to remember. Sea bridges stand on piles that grip hidden ground. A stiff, light deck lets a span reach farther. And a big number needs a denominator.

India built the bridge. Now the question is the city around it: what will the other end of the bridge become?

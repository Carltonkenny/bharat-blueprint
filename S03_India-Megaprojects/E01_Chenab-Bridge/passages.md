### P01

Look down into a gorge in Reasi, in Jammu and Kashmir. The river is a thin line, far below.

Now ask the engineer's question. How do you hold a railway line in the empty air above that river, across a gap that wide, for decades?

### P02

The Chenab Rail Bridge was inaugurated in June 2025. It is a steel arch bridge, one thousand three hundred and fifteen metres long. Its deck rises three hundred and fifty-nine metres above the riverbed, which the Press Information Bureau says is higher than the Eiffel Tower. It cost about one thousand four hundred and eighty-six crore rupees.

Hold on to two numbers. Three hundred and fifty-nine metres is how high it stands. Four hundred and sixty-seven metres is how wide the arch itself spans. They are different problems.

### P03

There is a reason to care that is not about height. The bridge is part of the Udhampur-Srinagar-Baramulla rail link. With a Vande Bharat train, the journey from Katra to Srinagar takes about three hours, two to three hours less than before.

### P04

Idea one: the arch. Think of two people standing close, leaning their hands against each other. Each pushes sideways into the other, and that sideways push keeps both of them up.

An arch works the same way. The weight pressing down is turned into a push along the curve. At the two ends, that push goes sideways into the hillsides. So the rock has to push back, and the gorge walls become part of the bridge.

Before the arch, a gap is only a gap. After it, the hills are carrying the load.

### P05

Idea two: building across empty air. You cannot put scaffolding on the bottom of a gorge this deep. So long steel arches are often built outward from both sides, each half held in place until the two meet in the middle.

Think of two people reaching across a stream to shake hands. Each reaches as far as they can, held from behind. Only when the hands meet does the arch hold itself.

### P06

Idea three: standing still in the wind. A tall, slim structure in strong wind does not simply stand there. It moves a little. Think of a tree in a storm: it survives because it can flex and come back.

The Press Information Bureau describes the Chenab bridge as designed to withstand strong wind and earthquake forces. Before the design, those forces are guesses. After it, they are numbers the steel has to carry every day.

### P07

Now, what India built, and what a bridge alone cannot do. The Chenab bridge is one crucial segment of that rail link. The same project's Anji Khad bridge, inaugurated the same month, is India's first cable-stayed railway bridge.

One bridge is not a railway. A route is only as strong as its hardest link. This was one of the hardest.

### P08

Bridges are not ranked by height alone. A high bridge, a long bridge, a wide-span bridge and a heavy-load bridge each solve a different problem. The Chenab bridge is high, with a long arch. Later in this series, you will meet a bridge that is long across the sea. These are different measures, not a league table.

### P09

A fictional morning: wind is building in the gorge, and a train is on its way. Somewhere, a rule must say when the train slows or stops. A bridge is steel, and it is also a set of rules for the people who run it.

### P10

One idea about people: anchoring. "Taller than the Eiffel Tower" sticks in the mind because we already know the tower. But a comparison is a shortcut. It tells you how high, not how hard. The hard part is the arch, the gorge and the wind.

### P11

Three things to remember. An arch turns a downward push into a sideways one, and the hills push back. Long arches are often built from both sides toward the middle. And a route is only as strong as its hardest link.

India built the bridge. Now the question is the route: where is the next gorge?

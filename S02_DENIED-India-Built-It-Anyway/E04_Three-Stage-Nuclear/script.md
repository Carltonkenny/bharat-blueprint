## Full spoken narration

On the sixth of April, twenty twenty-six, a reactor at Kalpakkam started a controlled chain reaction. It was built to do more than make electricity. It is meant to be a bridge to the reactors of the next generation. So what is a power station for, if its real job is to prepare the next one?

That is the unusual ambition behind India's three-stage nuclear power programme.

For a country seeking long-term energy security, the question was never simply how to build a reactor. It was how to make better use of available nuclear resources, develop difficult technologies at home, and create options for the future.

The engineering challenge was real. Nuclear fuel is not a magic stone that produces unlimited power.

Start with a campfire. A log releases stored chemical energy as it burns. A nuclear reactor releases energy through controlled changes inside atomic nuclei. That energy becomes heat. The heat can produce steam, the steam drives a turbine, and the turbine generates electricity. The turbine is familiar. The source of heat is not.

Now picture a farm that does more than harvest crops. It also produces seeds for the next season. It is only an analogy, but it captures the idea behind a breeder reactor. The system can convert some material into new usable nuclear fuel while it operates.

That does not mean a reactor creates energy from nothing. It means the fuel cycle is designed to make better use of material that would otherwise remain underused. The science, the materials and the safety systems are far more complex than the farming analogy.

India's long-term plan connects different reactor technologies in stages. The early stage established experience with pressurised heavy-water reactors. The next stage develops fast breeder technology. The eventual ambition is broader thorium utilisation.

And this is where the story becomes tangible.

India's indigenously designed Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor is rated at five hundred megawatts electric. In April twenty twenty-six it reached first criticality. According to the Department of Atomic Energy, this marked entry into the programme's second stage. First criticality means a controlled chain reaction has been established. It does not mean the reactor has completed every step needed for sustained commercial electricity generation.

Here is a fictional scenario. An energy planner imagines a future fleet of reactors. Building the first plant is only the beginning. The planner must also account for fuel preparation, maintenance, regulation, waste management and the next generation of facilities. The challenge is not one machine. It is an entire system across decades.

There is more work ahead. The third stage is still a long-term objective, not a fully deployed national power system. Safe operation, fuel-cycle infrastructure, cost and scale remain essential questions.

The world may judge a programme by a single ceremony. Engineers judge it by what works, what is safe, and what can be repeated.

The achievement deserves respect precisely because the remaining work is difficult. A milestone is not the end of the road. It tells engineers where the next road begins.

Some victories take generations to prove.

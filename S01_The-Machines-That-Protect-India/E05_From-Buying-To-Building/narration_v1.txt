We believe India buys its weapons. Some of it does. But look at what is being made. In Episode 4, we saw how a defence system senses, decides and steers. This time, we ask a plainer question: who makes the thing? Not who designs it. Who makes the thousands of parts, in thousands of workshops, and makes them well enough to trust? That is the story of how a country goes from buying to building.

There is a reason to care that is not about engineering. In twenty fourteen to fifteen, India's defence production was about forty-six thousand crore rupees. In twenty twenty-five to twenty-six, it was one point seven eight lakh crore. A lakh crore is a trillion rupees. So that is one point seven eight trillion rupees, a record, the government said in June.

To see where that money goes, we need three ideas. Idea one: many small makers make one machine. Think of a car. It is not built in one workshop. Hundreds of small shops make the seats, the glass and the screws, and one factory puts them together. A defence machine is the same. About sixteen thousand small and medium businesses are part of India's defence industry. Picture a web, with a big factory at the centre and small makers around it, each doing one thing well.

Idea two: nothing ships until it passes. We tell you this because a thousand parts are only as good as the worst one. Think of school exams. You do not move up because you tried. You move up because you passed. Every part is tested against a standard, and the whole machine is tested again. This is the proof idea from Episode 2, only now it runs in every workshop.

Idea three: making one is not the same as making a thousand. Why does that matter? Because a defence force needs thousands, on time. Think of baking. One cake at home is a hobby. A thousand cakes a day is a bakery, with ovens, shifts and a steady supply of flour. To scale, a country needs factories, trained workers and a steady flow of orders. In twenty twenty-four to twenty-five, the government signed a record one hundred and ninety-three defence contracts, worth more than two lakh crore rupees, and about ninety-two percent went to domestic industry.

Now, the part that is not engineering. In twenty twenty-five to twenty-six, India's defence exports reached a record thirty-eight thousand four hundred and twenty-four crore rupees. In twenty fourteen, the same exports were under two thousand crore. That is more than nineteen times as much. Here the small-base effect matters. When you start from almost nothing, any growth looks enormous. So hold two numbers together. Exports are about a fifth of what India produces. That is real growth from a small start, and still a long way from the whole.

What India can do: produce at a record level, with thousands of small makers, and, the Defence Minister says, reach more than eighty countries. What it cannot yet do: reach its own targets. The aim for twenty twenty-nine is three lakh crore in production and fifty thousand crore in exports. A target is not a result. Between today and that aim, there are about three years.

Think back to the cake in Episode 2. A country can buy the cake, bake from someone else's recipe, write a recipe together, or write its own. Countries often mix all four, for different parts, at different times. Building more at home does not mean buying nothing. It means choosing what to buy, and why.

A fictional Sunday: a small part fails on a ship far from port. If a workshop nearby makes that part, a replacement can arrive by morning. If not, it waits for a delivery. A factory floor is part of the defence. It just does not look like one.

One idea about people: we trust big multiples. Nineteen times sounds like a victory. The honest question is always: nineteen times what? Ask it of every record you hear, including the ones in this series.

Five episodes, one chain. An air defence system, a fighter engine, a floating airport, a defence that senses and decides, and the workshops under all of them. In each one, India has built a great deal, and has something left to build. India has learned to build. The question for the next decade: what will it choose never to buy again?

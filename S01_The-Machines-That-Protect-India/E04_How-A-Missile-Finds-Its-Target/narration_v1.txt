We believe a missile is something a country simply buys. It is not. A missile is a decision made in seconds, by a machine, that you will never see. In Episode 3, a carrier carried the jets. This time, we look at the thing that keeps both of them safe. How does a missile find its target?

There is a reason to care that is not about engineering. In July nineteen eighty-three, India began a programme to build guided missiles of its own. The Ministry of Defence itself lists denial of critical technologies and import embargoes among the reasons it ran late. One of its missiles, Akash, was sanctioned at about ninety-five crore rupees in nineteen eighty-three. By two thousand seven, the sanction was five hundred and forty-eight crore. That is more than five times the cost, and a completion date that slipped from nineteen ninety-five to two thousand seven.

To hit something, you must first know where it is. That is idea one: sense. Think of shouting across a valley. You hear your own voice come back, and the delay tells you how far away the cliff is. A radar does the same, with radio waves instead of a shout. It sends out a pulse, waits for the echo, and measures the time. Before the echo, there is only empty sky. After it, there is a point on a screen: a place, and a direction.

But knowing is not enough. You must aim where it will be. That is idea two: decide. Think of catching a ball. You do not reach for where the ball is. You move to where it will land. A missile's computer does the same sum, again and again, many times a second, as the target moves. Before the sum, the missile only knows the past. After it, it has a path through the future.

And a decision is useless until the body obeys. That is idea three: steer. Think of steering a bicycle. A small turn of the handlebar, and the whole bike leans into the curve. A missile also steers with small movements. Air pushes on them, and the whole missile turns. Sense, decide, steer. Three jobs, repeated until the missile and its target meet.

Now, India. Under that same programme, India designed Trishul, Akash and Nag, three missiles for three different jobs. That is what India can do: design, build and test missile families of its own. What it cannot yet do is finish the shield. Mission Sudarshan Chakra, announced in August twenty twenty-five, aims at a national security shield by twenty thirty-five. A government statement describes it as a multi-level system with three layers of protection. An aim is not an achievement. It is a deadline with a name.

Think of finding a friend in the dark. You can call out and listen for the echo. That is how radar works. You can look for their body heat. That is how a heat seeker works. Or you can watch for a bright jacket. That is how a camera guides. Each way works best in some conditions and worst in others. These are tools, not ranks.

A fictional afternoon: a radar screen shows a dot where no dot should be. Within seconds the system must decide: friend, or not. A shield is not one machine. It is a chain, and every link must answer in time.

One idea about people: hindsight bias. After you know the answer, it always looks obvious. You say you knew it all along. Looking back, the road from denial to design looks like a straight line. At the time, nobody could be sure it would reach the end. So when you hear about a shield by twenty thirty-five, remember how uncertain the first road was.

Three things to remember. A programme begun in nineteen eighty-three, under denial, is the base of today's shield. A missile does three jobs: sense, decide, steer. And a shield is a chain, so its weakest link sets its strength. India built the missiles. Now the question is the chain: which link will be finished last?

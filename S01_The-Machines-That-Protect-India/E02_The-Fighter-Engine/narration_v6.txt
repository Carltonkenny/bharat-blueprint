Here is a question. India can design and build a fighter jet. So why is the engine inside it so hard to build? Think of a car: a beautiful body is useless without a motor, and a fighter jet is the same. An engine does one simple thing. It throws air backwards, very fast, so the aircraft moves forward, like a balloon you let go.

There is also a reason beyond engineering. A country that cannot build its own engines must ask someone else for them, and for repairs, and for delivery dates. In November twenty twenty-five, India's HAL signed for one hundred and thirteen more American engines for the Tejas, arriving between twenty twenty-seven and twenty thirty-two. A fighter order is also an engine order.

India tried this before. Its first home-built fighter, the Marut, flew in nineteen sixty-one, led by a German designer, Kurt Tank. But it never got the more powerful engine it was designed for.

Idea one: squeeze. First, the engine pulls in air. Then it squeezes it. Think of a bicycle pump: the more you press, the harder the air pushes back. Rows of spinning blades do this, stage after stage. The catch: imagine a crowd squeezing through a door. If one person stumbles, everyone jams. One flawed blade does the same to the airflow, and the engine stalls.

Idea two: heat. Next, fuel is sprayed into the squeezed air and burned, like a gas stove, only fiercer. The hot gas rushes past more blades, which spin the engine. But this gas is hotter than the metal would survive, like a spoon left in a candle flame. Two tricks help. Each blade is grown as a single crystal, like a chocolate bar in one piece, with no joins where cracks begin. And cool air flows from tiny holes, wrapping each blade in a cool blanket.

Idea three: proof. You would not trust a new bicycle brake after one try. An engine runs on a test stand, then in a chamber that copies thin air, then on a flying test aircraft. Only after thousands of hours does anyone trust it with a pilot.

Now, India. In nineteen eighty-nine, India began building its own fighter engine, Kaveri. It built nine prototypes, ran over three thousand test hours, and flew one on a test aircraft, a first for India. But it fell short of the thrust the Tejas needed, like a kite built for a lighter wind, so the Tejas flies on American F404 engines. Was the work wasted? No: its lessons now feed a version of the engine for an unmanned jet. Was the goal reached? Also no. Both are true.

Think of baking a cake. Some countries buy the cake. Some bake from someone else's recipe, called licence-building. Some write a recipe together. A few have written their own for decades. India has done the first three: it buys the F404, builds the AL-31FP under licence, and has announced a joint engine with France. These are stages of one climb, not a league table.

A fictional morning: an alert sounds, and fighters wait on a runway. The engines must start, perform, and bring the aircraft home. Spares matter too. If only four of six aircraft have working engines, only four fly tomorrow.

One idea about people: sunk cost. You paid for a movie ticket, the film is boring, and you stay because you paid. The money is gone either way. About two thousand crore rupees went into Kaveri. A crore is ten million, so that is about twenty billion rupees. It cannot decide the next step. Only what the next rupee buys matters.

India built the fighter. The engine is the harder half. Whose name will be on it?

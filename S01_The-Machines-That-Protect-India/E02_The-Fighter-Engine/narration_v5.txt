A fighter jet is the part you photograph. The engine is the part you wait for. An engine throws air backwards, very fast, so the aircraft moves forward, like a balloon you let go. India's first fighter, the Marut, flew in nineteen sixty-one without the engine it was designed for.

Idea one: squeeze. The engine pulls in air and squeezes it. Think of a bicycle pump: the more you press, the harder the air pushes back. Rows of spinning blades do this, stage after stage. The catch: imagine a crowd squeezing through a door. If one person stumbles, everyone jams. One flawed blade does the same to the airflow, and the engine stalls.

Idea two: heat. Fuel is sprayed into the squeezed air and burned, like a gas stove, only fiercer. The gas is hotter than the metal blades it hits would survive, like a spoon left in a candle flame. Two tricks help. Each blade is grown as a single crystal, like a chocolate bar in one piece, with no joins where cracks begin. And cool air flows from tiny holes, wrapping each blade in a cool blanket.

Idea three: proof. You would not trust a new bicycle brake after one try. An engine runs thousands of hours on a test stand, in an altitude chamber and on a flying test aircraft, before anyone trusts it.

Now, India. In nineteen eighty-nine, India began building its own fighter engine, Kaveri. It built nine prototypes, ran over three thousand test hours, and flew one on a test aircraft, a first for India. But it fell short of the thrust the Tejas needed, like a kite built for a lighter wind, so the Tejas flies on American F404 engines. Was the work wasted? No: its lessons now feed a version of the engine for an unmanned jet. Was the goal reached? Also no. Both are true.

Think of baking a cake. Some countries buy the cake. Some bake from someone else's recipe, called licence-building. Some write a recipe together. A few have written their own for decades. India has done the first three: it buys the F404, builds the AL-31FP under licence, and has announced a joint engine with France. Stages of one long climb, not a league table.

A fictional morning: an alert sounds, and fighters wait on a runway. The engine must start, perform, and bring the aircraft home. Spares matter too. If only four of six aircraft have working engines, only four fly tomorrow.

One idea about people: sunk cost. You paid for a movie ticket, the film is boring, and you stay because you paid. The money is gone either way. About two thousand crore rupees went into Kaveri. That cannot decide the next step. Only what the next rupee buys matters.

India built the fighter. The engine is the harder half. Whose name will be on it?

A fighter is the part you photograph. The engine is the part you wait for. India's Marut flew in nineteen sixty-one, and never got the engine it was designed for. A handsome airframe, waiting politely for its heart.

Idea one: squeeze. A compressor crushes air, stage after stage. One flawed blade, and it stalls. Physics is unsentimental.

Idea two: heat. The gas burns hotter than the metal it strikes would melt. Single-crystal blades and a skin of cooling air keep them solid. Persuading metal to survive that is most of the job.

Idea three: proof. An engine is thousands of test-stand hours before anyone trusts it in the sky.

Now India. What it can do: Kaveri gave it nine prototype engines, over three thousand test hours, and a flight on a test aircraft. What it cannot yet do: power Tejas. Kaveri fell short of the thrust Tejas needed, so Tejas flies on American engines. Was the work wasted? No. Was the goal reached? Also no. Both can be true. Common in engineering. Rare in press releases.

No league table, please. A few nations have built fighter engines across many generations. China joined them later. Others, India included, buy, licence-build, or co-develop. Different stages of one very long climb.

A fiction: an alert, fighters on a runway. Engines decide who launches, who performs, who comes home. Spares decide who goes again tomorrow.

One human habit: sunk cost, finishing a thing because you already paid for it. About two thousand crore rupees went into Kaveri. The past spend is no reason to continue, nor to stop. Only what the next rupee buys matters.

India built the fighter. The engine is the harder half. Whose name will be on it?
